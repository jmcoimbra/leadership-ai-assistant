# Engineering Career Skills

Distilled doctrine for coaching engineers on career growth. Sections directly applicable to leadership coaching: Career framing, Learning, Productivity. Financial / Fitness / Spirit are outside leadership coaching scope and skipped.

## Section 1 — Career

**Core thesis:** A software developer's career IS a business. Employer = customer. Most engineers think like indentured servants and let the role define them. The shift to business-mindset enables active career management.

### Ch 2 — Career as a Business

- **Mindset shift:** From "I'm an employee of a company" to "I run a business; my employer is my biggest customer." This shift is not metaphorical — it changes how you make career decisions.
- **What a business needs:** product/service (developing software), pricing, marketing, customer acquisition, ongoing improvement.
- **Practical questions:**
  - What is my service in one sentence?
  - How do I differentiate it from thousands of other developers?
  - How do I attract customers vs. cold-applying?

### Ch 3 — Goals

- Most engineers drift. They take the first job, stay until laid off.
- **Big goal + smaller goals + tracking.** Big goal is directional, not specific. Smaller goals are specific and time-bound (yearly → monthly → weekly → daily).
- Track at intervals matching the goal cadence. Course-correct.

### Ch 4 — People Skills

- Most of an engineer's time is spent dealing with people, not computers. Code is written first for human consumption.
- **Everyone wants to feel important.** Diminishing someone's contribution triggers oxygen-cut-off response.
- **Never criticize.** Especially toxic in Slack / public review.
- **Think about what the other person wants.** Translate your asks into their incentives.
- **Avoid arguments.** You can't win an argument. Even if you "win" you've made an enemy.

### Ch 5 — Hacking the Interview

- **Build rapport BEFORE the interview.** Get a referral. Be known.
- The actual interview is mostly chemistry, not technical Q&A. The technical bar is necessary but not sufficient.

### Ch 6 — Employment Options

Three paths: employee, independent consultant, entrepreneur. Each has distinct economics and freedom tradeoffs.

### Ch 7 — Specialization > Generalist

- **Most engineers specialize too broadly.** "I'm a Java developer" is too broad. "I specialize in Java backend services for high-throughput payment systems" is sharp.
- **Specialization narrows the pool but increases hit rate.** "You only need one client / one job."
- The Polyglot programmer can ALSO have a specialty. Wide skills + sharp specialty is the ideal.

### Ch 8 — Not All Companies Are Equal

Small / medium / large company tradeoffs. Small = high impact, low stability. Medium = stability, moderate pace. Large = process, training opportunities, world-changing scale.

### Ch 9 — Climbing the Corporate Ladder (CRITICAL FOR STAFF PREP)

Four levers, in order:

1. **Take responsibility.** Money catches up to responsibility. Always take more responsibility when offered. Hunt for neglected areas no one else wants — turn swamplands into fertile ground.
2. **Become visible.** specific tactic: weekly report to manager. "Daily log → Friday email summary." Manager hears about the work; report appears more productive than peers. Also: offer presentations, lunch-and-learns, speak up in meetings.
3. **Educate yourself.** Continuous learning. Certifications, courses, books. Beyond software development too — leadership, management, business.
4. **Be the problem solver.** "People who can't be told why something can't work are a dime a dozen." Be the person who has solutions and executes them. Politics matter least.

### Ch 10 — Being a Professional

Professional vs amateur table:

| Professional | Amateur |
|---|---|
| Has principles they abide by | Does whatever is asked |
| Focused on getting the job done RIGHT | Focused on getting the job done |
| Admits when wrong or doesn't know | Pretends to know |
| Consistent and stable | Unpredictable and unreliable |
| Takes responsibility | Avoids responsibility |

- **Habits are the foundation.** Pre-prepared standup statement, time-management routines, regular self-check-ins.
- **Doing what is right** even at personal cost. The principle of saying No — the software developer-as-doctor analogy.

### Ch 11-18 — Career Independence Topics

Quitting / freelancing / first product / startup / remote / fake-it-till-you-make-it / resumes / non-religious about technology. Tangentially applicable to retention coaching ("should I leave?") but not Staff prep priority.

## Section 2 — Marketing Yourself

**Core thesis:** Engineers undervalue self-marketing. The best engineers don't market; the second-tier engineers do, and they get the senior roles. pitch is for engineers to build a brand: blog, social, books, talks.

For Staff prep, external brand-building is secondary. Internal visibility (Section 1 Ch 9) matters more. But for the IC's quality-authority positioning, conference talks or external posts about test infrastructure could amplify the internal authority. Mark as optional path, not mandatory.

## Section 3 — Learning

### Ch 27-30 — The 10-Step Learning Process

system for self-teaching a new technology fast.

**Steps 1-6 (do once per topic):**
1. Get the big picture
2. Determine scope
3. Define success
4. Find resources
5. Create a learning plan
6. Filter resources

**Steps 7-10 (repeat per module):**
7. Learn enough to get started
8. Play around (build something broken on purpose)
9. Learn enough to do something useful
10. Teach (forces real understanding)

### Ch 31 — Find a Mentor

- Mentor qualities: domain expertise, willingness to teach, accessibility.
- Where to find: existing professional network, conferences, online presence.
- Recruit them by being explicit about ask.

### Ch 32-33 — Be a Mentor / Teach

- Teaching is the highest form of learning. The act of explaining to a less-experienced person forces clarity.
- Take an apprentice. Pick someone "worthy" — willing to do the work.

### Ch 34 — Degree Required?

Not for senior roles. Demonstrated work outweighs degree at scale.

### Ch 35 — Finding Gaps in Your Knowledge

- We avoid topics where we know we're weak. The avoidance compounds the gap.
- Find gaps by attempting to teach (Step 10 again) or by trying to apply knowledge outside the comfort zone.

## Section 4 — Productivity

### Ch 36 — Focus

- Focus is the multiplier on every other productivity tactic.
- Eliminate distractions; create blocks.

### Ch 37 — Personal Productivity Plan

Quarterly → Monthly → Weekly → Daily nested planning. Each tier informs the next.

### Ch 38 — Pomodoro Technique

25-minute focused work + 5-minute break. Pomodoro is a unit of attention. Track Pomodoros completed per day as the productivity metric, not hours worked.

### Ch 39 — Quota System

- Set a daily / weekly quota for output. example: write X words per day for blog. Books shipped per quarter.
- Quotas force consistency. Inspiration is unreliable; quotas are reliable.

### Ch 40 — Holding Yourself Accountable

- Internal accountability is hard. External accountability (a partner, a coach, a public commitment) works better.

### Ch 41 — Multitasking

- Multitasking is bad. Batching is much more productive. True multitasking is a myth except for asocial / mechanical tasks.

### Ch 42 — Burnout

- Burnout = hitting a wall. Push through (a little) to find what's on the other side.
- Real burnout requires structural relief: time off, scope change, role change.
